Sentence Structure

Learn to arrange words into complete, accurate and purposeful sentences.

Key idea

Clear sentences are built around a subject, a verb, and a complete idea that makes sense.

Parts of a Sentence

A complete sentence expresses a complete thought and begins with a capital letter. It ends with a full stop, question mark or exclamation mark.

The subject tells who or what the sentence is about. The predicate tells what the subject does or is.


The hardworking pupils cleaned the classroom.

Subject: The hardworking pupils. Predicate: cleaned the classroom.



Types of Sentences

A declarative sentence gives information: “The lesson starts at eight.” An interrogative sentence asks a question: “When does the lesson start?” An imperative gives a command or request: “Open your book.” An exclamatory sentence expresses strong feeling: “What a wonderful result!”



Simple and Compound Sentences

A simple sentence has one main idea: “Kofi studies.” A compound sentence joins two complete ideas with a coordinating conjunction such as and, but, or, so.


Kofi studies, and Adwoa practises.

Use a comma before the joining conjunction when it connects two complete sentences.



Subject-Verb Agreement

A singular subject usually takes a singular verb: “The girl runs.” A plural subject takes the base verb: “The girls run.” Be careful with words between the subject and verb.


Correct: The basket of mangoes is heavy.



Interactive Check

Which sentence is correct?

A. The boys plays football.

B. The boys play football.

B is correct because the plural subject “boys” takes “play.”



Remember

Every sentence needs a complete thought. Check its type, punctuation, word order and subject-verb agreement.
